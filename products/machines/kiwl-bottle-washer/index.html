Why stage count is the specification that matters

Label removal and mould kill both need soak time at temperature. An eight-stage machine handles lightly soiled bottles from a closed loop; a 24-stage machine handles bottles returning from warm distribution with dried residue. Buying fewer stages than the soil level requires is the most common cause of a washer that never reaches its rated output.

Caustic recovery changes the operating cost

Caustic is the largest consumable in a washer. A recovery loop with filtration returns usable caustic to the bath and cuts fresh chemical by 20-30 percent, which typically pays for the option within two years on a 12,000 BPH machine.

Counter-current rinse for water use

Fresh water enters at the final rinse and flows backwards through the stages. This is what brings consumption under 0.6 L per bottle, against 1.5-2 L on a once-through rinse.

Typical Applications

  • Breweries with returnable fleets — Returnable glass only pays if the washer keeps label and mould rejection low enough for the bottles to be refilled.
  • Bottlers in markets with deposit schemes — Deposit systems return heavily soiled bottles; the higher stage count matters more than throughput here.
  • Breweries cutting water cost — Counter-current rinse and caustic recovery together cut water and chemical spend by a third.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much caustic does it consume?

Roughly 0.2-0.4 kg per 1,000 bottles with recovery, against 0.5-0.7 kg without.

Can it handle one-way bottles?

Yes, but a one-way bottle normally only needs a rinser. A full washer is only economic with returnable glass.

What maintenance does the chain need?

Chain lubrication weekly, pocket inspection monthly, and annual chain tension and wear measurement.
