Why the cone angle matters

A 60 degree cone lets yeast and trub slide to the bottom outlet without a manual push, which is what makes harvesting reproducible. A shallower cone is cheaper to build but leaves a yeast shelf that has to be washed out, and that shelf is where off-flavours start.

Cooling jacket layout versus fermentation curve

Two zones suit ales where the temperature profile is gentle; three zones give the control that lagers need during the slow diacetyl rest. Running glycol at 2 degrees C rather than -4 degrees C reduces the risk of icing the jacket wall and cracking the weld.

Pressure rating and carbonation

If you plan to carbonate in the tank rather than in a bright tank, specify 3 bar. At 1.5 bar you are limited to conditioning, and any attempt to force-carbonate will lift the relief valve.

Typical Applications

  • Breweries matching tank to brew day — Plan roughly one fermenter per weekly brew plus one or two spares, so a stuck fermentation does not stop the brewhouse.
  • Dry-hopped and sour programmes — A dedicated dry-hop port and a 74 degree cone for sours keep these beers off the main tanks.
  • Breweries harvesting yeast — A racking arm above the cone lets you crop clean yeast and dump trub separately.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many fermenters do I need for a 1,000 L brewhouse?

Four to six for weekly brewing plus conditioning, assuming a 2-3 week turnaround per batch.

Can tanks be stacked?

Up to 2,000 L they can be stacked two high with a suitable frame, which halves the cellar footprint.

Is 316L necessary?

For standard beer, 304 is sufficient. Choose 316L for high-chloride water, sour programmes or where a customer audit specifies it.
